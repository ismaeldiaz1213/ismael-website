<a id="tldr"></a>
> **TL;DR:** At a wedding in Cana, the wine ran out. Mary brought the problem to Jesus, the servants filled six stone jars with water, and Jesus quietly turned 100+ gallons of it into the best wine of the feast. It was His very first miracle (John 2:11). It showed He's the Creator, and it made His brand-new disciples' faith stronger. The application: Jesus takes ordinary people, like plain water in plain jars, and makes them new. All it takes is going to Him.

## About the Miracle

<a id="introduction"></a>
### Introduction

I've taught this story to the kids on my bus route in Houston and to the 4 and 5 year old class I used to teach in Durham. Every time, the same thing strikes me: nobody saw this coming.

For about thirty years, Jesus lived in Nazareth as "the carpenter" (Mark 6:3). People ate with Him, worked with Him, and watched Him grow up. Then one afternoon at a small-town wedding, ordinary water came out of stone jars as the best wine anybody had tasted all week. It wasn't a sermon in the synagogue or a healing in front of a crowd. It was a quiet miracle at a family celebration, and most of the guests never even knew it happened. But John tells us it was the *beginning*, the first sign that showed who Jesus really was.

In this study we'll walk through John 2:1–11 verse by verse in the King James Version. We'll look at the history behind it, lean on some solid Baptist teachers, be honest about the questions Christians still discuss, and finish with what it means for your life.

Because honestly: you think you know a guy, and then all of a sudden he's able to turn water into wine!

> **John 2:11**: *This beginning of miracles did Jesus in Cana of Galilee, and manifested forth his glory; and his disciples believed on him.* [[12]](#ref-12)

<a id="background"></a>
### Historical and Cultural Background

#### Where it falls in John's Gospel

John carefully counts the days leading up to Cana:

1. **John 1:19**: Priests and Levites question John the Baptist.
2. **John 1:29**: "The next day," John points to Jesus: "Behold the Lamb of God."
3. **John 1:35**: "Again the next day," Andrew and another disciple follow Jesus, and Andrew brings Peter.
4. **John 1:43**: "The day following," Jesus calls Philip, and Philip brings Nathanael.
5. **John 2:1**: "The third day," the wedding at Cana.

So Jesus shows up with a brand-new group of followers: Andrew, Peter, Philip, Nathanael, and probably John himself. These men had just confessed Him as Messiah (John 1:41, 45, 49).

Fun detail: just a few days earlier, Nathanael had said, "Can there any good thing come out of Nazareth?" (John 1:46). Now he gets to watch the guy from Nazareth make the best wine in his own hometown. I'd love to have seen his face.

#### Cana of Galilee

Cana was a small village in the hills of Galilee, not far from Nazareth. It was Nathanael's hometown (John 21:2), and Jesus came back there later to heal a nobleman's son from a distance (John 4:46–54). The name likely comes from a Hebrew word meaning "place of reeds." [[11]](#ref-11)

Nobody knows for certain where Cana stood. Four sites have been proposed: [[10]](#ref-10)

| Site | Where | The case for it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Kafr Kanna | About 4 miles northeast of Nazareth | Traditional pilgrim site with the "Wedding Church"; recent Israel Antiquities Authority digs at Karm er-Ras, on its west side, argue for a first-century village [[9]](#ref-9) |
| Khirbet Qana | About 8 miles north of Nazareth | Jewish village in Jesus' day; early Christian cave shrine; fits ancient pilgrim accounts and Josephus [[8]](#ref-8) |
| Reineh | Near Nazareth | Minor candidate |
| Qana | Southern Lebanon | Older tradition; most scholars place Cana in Lower Galilee instead |

The exact spot doesn't change the story, but it's a good reminder that this happened in a real place among real people. Archaeologists are still digging.

#### A wedding in first-century Israel

Jewish marriages had two stages. First came the **betrothal**, a binding agreement. That's the stage Mary and Joseph were in when Mary was found with child (Matthew 1:18). Months later came the **wedding**: the groom led a procession to bring his bride home, followed by a feast.

That feast could last up to a week (compare Genesis 29:27 and Judges 14:12). Spurgeon described village weddings where the whole town came to eat and drink and the party went on a week or more. [[1]](#ref-1) The groom's family had to provide for all of it.

So running out of wine wasn't just awkward. It was a public embarrassment. Hospitality was a matter of family honor, and a failed feast was the kind of thing a small town talks about for years.

#### The six stone waterpots

John says the jars were there "after the manner of the purifying of the Jews." Jews washed hands, cups, and vessels before meals for ceremonial cleanliness (Mark 7:3–4). Stone was used because, under Jewish tradition, stone vessels didn't become ritually unclean the way clay pots could.

Each jar held "two or three firkins." A firkin (Greek *metretes*) was about 9 gallons, so each jar held roughly 18–27 gallons. All six together held around **108–162 gallons**. Spurgeon put it at no less than 120 gallons. [[1]](#ref-1)

#### The governor of the feast

The "governor" or "ruler of the feast" (Greek *architriklinos*) ran the banquet: managing the servants, tasting the food and drink, and keeping things moving. John Gill notes he may also have led the blessings over the cup that were said at Jewish weddings.

#### Wine in the ancient world

The Greek word in John 2 is *oinos*. It's a broad word that covered the juice of the grape in many forms, from freshly pressed juice to fully fermented wine. [[6]](#ref-6) Wine was an everyday drink at meals and a symbol of joy and blessing in the Old Testament (Psalm 104:15). What kind of wine Jesus made is one of the most discussed questions about this passage, so it gets its own spot in the [Mysteries](#mysteries) section.

### Verse by Verse: John 2:1–11 (KJV)

<a id="v1"></a>
#### Verse 1

> *And the third day there was a marriage in Cana of Galilee; and the mother of Jesus was there:*

**"The third day"** is counted from the last event in John 1, when Jesus called Philip and Nathanael (John 1:43). Gill notes it could be counted from a couple of different starting points in chapter 1, but the point is the same: this happened within days of Jesus gathering His first disciples. [[2]](#ref-2)

**"The mother of Jesus was there."** John never uses Mary's name in his Gospel; she's always "the mother of Jesus." She seems to be more than a guest. She notices the wine problem, she gives the servants instructions, and they actually listen to her. Gill suggests the wedding may have been for a relative of Mary's family. [[2]](#ref-2)

Joseph isn't mentioned here at all. In fact, the last time the Bible mentions him alive is when Jesus was twelve, at the temple (Luke 2:41–51). We don't know when he died, but by the time of Jesus' ministry he seems to be gone, and many teachers, Gill included, assume he had passed away. [[2]](#ref-2)

<a id="v2"></a>
#### Verse 2

> *And both Jesus was called, and his disciples, to the marriage.*

Jesus was **invited**. The Son of God didn't crash the party. Somebody wanted Him there. His new disciples came along too, which means the host suddenly had a handful of extra guests. That might not have helped the wine situation.

The first place Jesus works a miracle isn't a temple or a palace. It's a wedding. God created marriage in the garden (Genesis 2:24), and here the Lord Jesus honors it with His presence. The Bible even pictures the end of history as a wedding feast: "the marriage supper of the Lamb" (Revelation 19:9).

<a id="v3"></a>
#### Verse 3

> *And when they wanted wine, the mother of Jesus saith unto him, They have no wine.*

"Wanted" in old English means "lacked." The wine ran out. With a feast that could last days and the whole village invited, this was a real crisis for the groom's family. Somewhere in that house, somebody was quietly panicking.

Mary doesn't give orders or try to fix it herself. She simply brings the need to Jesus: **"They have no wine."** That's a model of prayer. She tells Him the problem and leaves the solution to Him.

What did she expect? The Bible doesn't say. She had never seen Him do a miracle; verse 11 says this was the first. But she knew who He was. She remembered the angel's words and had "kept all these things" in her heart for thirty years (Luke 2:19, 51).

<a id="v4"></a>
#### Verse 4

> *Jesus saith unto her, Woman, what have I to do with thee? mine hour is not yet come.*

This verse sounds harsh to modern ears, but it isn't.

**"Woman"** was a respectful way to address a lady. Jesus uses the same word from the cross when He lovingly puts Mary in John's care: "Woman, behold thy son!" (John 19:26). Gill points out it was a common, respectful address among the Jews. [[3]](#ref-3)

**"What have I to do with thee?"** is a Hebrew expression (see 1 Kings 17:18; 2 Kings 3:13). It means something like, "What do you and I have in common in this matter?" Gill explains that while Jesus had been subject to Mary as her son (Luke 2:51), in His work as Messiah He took His direction from His Father, not from her. [[3]](#ref-3) It's a gentle but clear correction. Baptists have always noticed that this verse gives no support to the idea that Mary directs or controls her Son.

**"Mine hour is not yet come."** Throughout John, Jesus' "hour" points to His death and glorification (John 7:30; 8:20; 12:23; 13:1; 17:1). Gill understood it here as the time for His public miracles. He thought Jesus was willing to help but would do it quietly, on His Father's schedule. [[3]](#ref-3) Either way, Jesus runs on God's clock.

<a id="v5"></a>
#### Verse 5

> *His mother saith unto the servants, Whatsoever he saith unto you, do it.*

I love this. Jesus says "mine hour is not yet come," and Mary basically goes, "Okay. Anyway, servants, do whatever He tells you." Every mom reading this knows exactly that move.

These are **Mary's last recorded words in the Bible**, and they point away from herself to her Son. **"Whatsoever he saith unto you, do it"** is still the best advice anyone can give. It echoes Pharaoh's words about Joseph in Egypt: "Go unto Joseph; what he saith to you, do" (Genesis 41:55). Obedience comes before the miracle.

<a id="v6"></a>
#### Verse 6

> *And there were set there six waterpots of stone, after the manner of the purifying of the Jews, containing two or three firkins apiece.*

John slows down to describe the jars. These weren't wine jars; they were for **ceremonial washing**. Each held about 18–27 gallons.

Spurgeon noticed that Jesus didn't send for special containers. He used what was already there, plain stone jars sitting in the corner of the house. [[1]](#ref-1) That's how He still works.

Many Bible teachers see a picture here. Water for religious washing could never change anything inside a person. Jesus takes those jars and fills them with something new. John already told us: "the law was given by Moses, but grace and truth came by Jesus Christ" (John 1:17).

<a id="v7"></a>
#### Verse 7

> *Jesus saith unto them, Fill the waterpots with water. And they filled them up to the brim.*

Put yourself in the servants' sandals. The wine is gone, the party's in trouble, and the guy Mary vouched for says... get water? I imagine at least one servant looking at the other like, "We're out of wine and He wants us to do dishes?"

And somebody definitely did the math on how many trips to the well 120+ gallons is.

Spurgeon preached a whole sermon on this verse, "The Waterpots at Cana," and pulled out several lessons: [[1]](#ref-1)

- **Christ usually gives a command before He gives a blessing.** "Fill the waterpots" came before the wine, just as "Stretch forth thine hand" came before healing and "Believe on the Lord Jesus Christ" comes before salvation.
- **His commands aren't to be questioned, but obeyed,** even when they don't make sense yet. The servants needed wine and were told to haul water.
- **Obey Him all the way, "up to the brim."** They didn't fill the jars halfway.
- **Our work alone can't do it.** Full jars of water are still just water until Jesus speaks.

Filling the jars to the brim also removed any doubt. There was no room left to sneak wine in. Spurgeon compared it to Elijah soaking the altar on Mount Carmel so no one could claim a trick (1 Kings 18:33–35). [[1]](#ref-1)

<a id="v8"></a>
#### Verse 8

> *And he saith unto them, Draw out now, and bear unto the governor of the feast. And they bare it.*

Now the step of faith. Jesus doesn't pray out loud, wave His hand, or make an announcement. No lightning, no music. Just: "Draw out now." Honestly, Jesus is so calm about it. It's not that deep, guys. Just go.

Meanwhile, imagine being the servant holding that cup. You *know* you just poured water into that jar. Now you're carrying a cup of it to the most important man at the feast. I picture him sweating the whole walk over, rehearsing his apology: "Sir, I can explain..." Somewhere between the jar and the governor's lips, the water became wine.

Faith often looks like carrying what you have to where Jesus told you to take it, and letting Him handle the rest.

<a id="v9"></a>
#### Verse 9

> *When the ruler of the feast had tasted the water that was made wine, and knew not whence it was: (but the servants which drew the water knew;) the governor of the feast called the bridegroom,*

**"The water that was made wine."** No grapes, no vines, no time. Spurgeon put it beautifully: every year God makes wine by sending rain down to the roots of the vine and into the grapes. At Cana, Jesus just did in a moment what He normally does over a season. [[1]](#ref-1) That's the Creator at work (John 1:3).

Notice who knew. The important man, the governor, had no idea where it came from. **The servants knew.** I bet they were trying very hard to keep a straight face. Spurgeon applied it this way: the people who do the work of serving Christ, the ones filling the waterpots, are the ones who get to see His power up close. [[1]](#ref-1)

<a id="v10"></a>
#### Verse 10

> *And saith unto him, Every man at the beginning doth set forth good wine; and when men have well drunk, then that which is worse: but thou hast kept the good wine until now.*

The governor states the usual custom: serve the best first, then bring out the cheaper stuff later when nobody's paying attention. But this wine was **better** than anything served so far. Jesus doesn't do second-rate work.

And the groom gets complimented for wine he didn't buy. I like to imagine him just nodding slowly like, "Yeah... I planned that." That's grace, though: we get credit for a goodness that came from Him.

Gill applied "thou hast kept the good wine until now" to the gospel itself. God gave His truth in pieces through the Old Testament but saved the best, the full revelation of His Son, for last (Hebrews 1:1–2). And for the believer, the very best is still ahead in heaven. [[4]](#ref-4)

<a id="v11"></a>
#### Verse 11

> *This beginning of miracles did Jesus in Cana of Galilee, and manifested forth his glory; and his disciples believed on him.*

This is John's own summary, and it tells us three things.

1. **"This beginning of miracles."** This was the very first. That means the later legends about Jesus doing miracles as a boy aren't true, and Gill says plainly there's no reason to believe them. [[5]](#ref-5) For thirty years Jesus lived an ordinary life, which is exactly why this would have been so shocking.
2. **"Manifested forth his glory."** His glory, hidden behind an ordinary human life, shone through. John said earlier, "we beheld his glory, the glory as of the only begotten of the Father" (John 1:14). Gill calls it the glory of His deity breaking through. [[5]](#ref-5)
3. **"His disciples believed on him."** They already believed He was the Messiah (John 1:41, 49). Now their faith was confirmed and deepened. [[5]](#ref-5) That's the purpose of every sign in John: "these are written, that ye might believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God; and that believing ye might have life through his name" (John 20:31).

<a id="significance"></a>
### Why This Miracle Matters

This wasn't the biggest miracle Jesus ever did, but it was the first, and John put it at the front door of his Gospel on purpose.

#### The first of John's seven signs

John calls Jesus' miracles "signs" because each one points beyond itself to who Jesus is. He records seven before the resurrection:

| # | Sign | Passage | What it shows |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Water into wine | John 2:1–11 | Lord over creation; brings joy and new life |
| 2 | Nobleman's son healed | John 4:46–54 | Lord over distance |
| 3 | Lame man at Bethesda | John 5:1–9 | Lord over time and weakness |
| 4 | Feeding the 5,000 | John 6:1–14 | The Bread of Life |
| 5 | Walking on water | John 6:16–21 | Lord over nature |
| 6 | Man born blind | John 9:1–7 | The Light of the World |
| 7 | Lazarus raised | John 11:1–44 | The Resurrection and the Life |

The first two signs both happen in Cana, bookending the opening section of John's Gospel.

#### It shows Jesus is the Creator

John opens by saying "all things were made by him" (John 1:3). At Cana we see it. Only the One who made water and grapes in the first place could turn one into the other in an instant.

#### Moses turned water to blood; Jesus turned water to wine

Spurgeon pointed out that Moses' first public plague turned water into blood, a sign of judgment (Exodus 7:19–20). Jesus begins His ministry by turning water into wine, a sign of generosity and joy. [[1]](#ref-1) The law brought death; Christ brings life and grace (John 1:17; 2 Corinthians 3:6).

#### The old is replaced by something better

The jars were for ritual washing, a system of outward cleansing that could never change a heart. Jesus fills them with something new. The rest of John 2 drives it home: right after Cana, Jesus cleanses the temple and speaks of His own body as the true temple (John 2:13–21).

#### Abundance and joy

The prophets pictured the days of the Messiah as a time when wine would flow freely (Amos 9:13; Joel 3:18; Isaiah 25:6). Over a hundred gallons of the best wine is not a stingy gift. Spurgeon said Jesus gives like a king, not just enough to get by, but grace upon grace (John 1:16). [[1]](#ref-1)

#### The best is saved for last

"Thou hast kept the good wine until now" fits the whole story of salvation. God's best gift came last: His Son. And for believers, the very best is still coming at the marriage supper of the Lamb (Revelation 19:9).

<a id="mysteries"></a>
### Mysteries: Things to Be Curious About

The Bible tells us everything we need, but not everything we might wonder about. Here are questions Christians still discuss. None of them change the main point of the story, but they're worth thinking through honestly.

#### Assumptions vs. what the text actually says

| Common assumption | What John 2 actually says |
| --- | --- |
| Jesus spoke rudely to His mother | "Woman" was respectful; He used it lovingly from the cross (John 19:26) |
| Everyone at the wedding saw the miracle | Only the servants, Mary, and the disciples knew; the governor "knew not whence it was" |
| Jesus waved His hand or prayed over the jars | No words or gestures are recorded, only "Fill" and "Draw out now" |
| The jars were wine jars | They were for Jewish purification washing |
| Jesus did miracles growing up | This was "the beginning of miracles" |
| "The third day" means the third day of the feast | It counts from Jesus calling Philip and Nathanael (John 1:43) |
| Joseph died when Jesus was twelve | He's last *mentioned* then (Luke 2); when he died isn't recorded |

#### 1. What kind of wine did Jesus make?

This is the big one, and sincere Bible-believing Christians land in different places.

**The view held by many Independent Baptists:** the wine was fresh, unfermented grape juice. Those who hold this view argue:

- The Greek *oinos* is a general word that covered grape juice at any stage, fresh or fermented. The context decides which. [[6]](#ref-6)
- The Bible warns strongly against intoxicating wine (Proverbs 20:1; 23:29–32; Habakkuk 2:15). Jesus, who never sinned, would not hand more than 100 gallons of intoxicating drink to guests who had already "well drunk."
- What comes straight from the Creator's hand is pure. Jesus skipped the vine and the fermenting vat entirely. [[7]](#ref-7)
- This view has a long history among Baptists. William Patton's 1871 book *Bible Wines* argued at length that the Cana wine was non-alcoholic, and total-abstinence Baptists still cite it today. [[6]](#ref-6)

**Other views among Baptists and Bible-believers:**

- **Spurgeon** called it real wine, but said it was nothing like the strong, fortified wine of his day. In his view it was the mild drink of a soberer age that hurt no one. He also said he abstained from alcohol completely and encouraged others to do the same. [[1]](#ref-1)
- **John Gill** read "when men have well drunk" as drinking freely and cheerfully, but not to drunkenness. [[4]](#ref-4)
- Some Baptist writers say honestly that the text alone can't settle it either way, and that abstinence is still the wise course for believers today. [[7]](#ref-7)
- Many other Christians believe it was ordinary fermented wine, since it was called "good wine" in a setting where wine was normally fermented.

What everyone agrees on: it was a real miracle, the wine was the best at the feast, and nothing in this passage encourages drunkenness, which the Bible clearly condemns (Ephesians 5:18).

#### 2. Whose wedding was it?

The Bible never names the couple. Gill, following the scholar John Lightfoot, guessed it was a relative of Mary's family, since Mary seems to have some say in how things are run. [[2]](#ref-2) Others suggest a connection to Nathanael, who lived in Cana. Later traditions have offered names, but Scripture gives none.

#### 3. Did Mary expect a miracle?

She'd never seen Him do one. Was she expecting a miracle, asking Him to go buy more, or just telling Him the problem? Her instruction to the servants suggests she believed He'd do *something*. What she pictured, we don't know.

#### 4. If His hour hadn't come, why did He act anyway?

Jesus says "mine hour is not yet come," then helps anyway. Some explain that His "hour" means His death and glorification, not miracles in general. Gill thought Jesus was declining to act at His mother's direction or in a public way, but was willing to help quietly. [[3]](#ref-3) Others see Him making it clear that He acts on His Father's timing, and then acting when the Father's timing allowed.

#### 5. Where was Joseph?

Joseph isn't mentioned after the temple visit when Jesus was twelve (Luke 2:41–51). Most teachers assume he died sometime in the eighteen or so years between then and Cana. Jesus entrusting Mary to John at the cross (John 19:26–27) fits that. But the Bible never tells us when, or how.

#### 6. When exactly did the water change?

Did all six jars become wine at once, or only the water that was drawn out and carried to the governor? The text says the servants "drew the water" and the governor tasted "the water that was made wine," but it doesn't say exactly when or where the change happened. Most assume all six jars became wine.

#### 7. Do the numbers mean something?

Some Bible teachers see meaning in **six** jars (six is often linked to man and incompleteness, falling short of seven) or in **"the third day"** (a hint of the resurrection). Others count the days in John 1–2 and see a "new creation" week ending at Cana. These are interesting ideas, but John doesn't explain them, so it's wise to hold them loosely.

#### 8. Why such a "small" problem?

Of all the needs in Israel, sickness, blindness, death, why did Jesus start by solving a party problem? Some see it as a picture that He cares about everyday needs and family joys, not just emergencies. Others note it was the perfect quiet setting for a first sign meant mostly for His disciples. Probably both are true.

<a id="application"></a>
## Application

### He can turn your life into wine

This is the part I love most. Look at what Jesus started with: plain water, in plain stone jars, carried by ordinary (and probably confused) servants. Nothing special. And He turned it into the best thing at the whole feast.

That's what He does with people. He can take a simple person like you and me and turn it into something great. In that day, the great thing was wine. The beauty of how God works is that He doesn't need you to be impressive first. Paul put it this way: "we have this treasure in earthen vessels, that the excellency of the power may be of God, and not of us" (2 Corinthians 4:7).

Water can't make itself into wine. No matter how hard it tries, it's still water. And you can't make yourself new either. Church attendance, good works, and trying harder are like filling the jars. They're good things, but they're still water until Jesus speaks. Only He can change what's inside.

But here's the good news: **all it takes is for you to go to Him.**

1. **Admit you need Him.** "For all have sinned, and come short of the glory of God" (Romans 3:23). Like that wedding, we've run out, and we can't fix it ourselves.
2. **Believe He paid for your sin.** "But God commendeth his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8). He died, was buried, and rose again (1 Corinthians 15:3–4).
3. **Repent and turn to Him.** "Repent ye therefore, and be converted, that your sins may be blotted out" (Acts 3:19).
4. **Call on Him and ask Him into your heart.** "Behold, I stand at the door, and knock: if any man hear my voice, and open the door, I will come in to him" (Revelation 3:20). "For whosoever shall call upon the name of the Lord shall be saved" (Romans 10:13).

When you do, He turns your life into wine. "Therefore if any man be in Christ, he is a new creature: old things are passed away; behold, all things are become new" (2 Corinthians 5:17).

If you've never done that, you can do it right now, right where you are. Just talk to Him. Tell Him you're a sinner, that you believe He died and rose again for you, and ask Him to save you and come into your life.

### More lessons from Cana

- **Bring your needs to Jesus.** Mary didn't panic or try to fix it herself. She said, "They have no wine," and trusted Him with it. Nothing is too small to pray about.
- **Do whatever He says.** "Whatsoever he saith unto you, do it" is still the key to the Christian life. Read His Word and obey it, even when it doesn't make sense yet.
- **Fill the jars to the brim.** When Jesus gives a command, go all the way with it. Don't serve Him halfway. [[1]](#ref-1)
- **Do the ordinary work.** Teaching a Sunday school class, riding the bus route, inviting a neighbor: that's filling the waterpots with water. Jesus is the one who turns it into wine. And like those servants, the people who do the work get to see the miracle up close.
- **Trust His timing.** "Mine hour is not yet come" reminds us that Jesus works on His Father's schedule, not ours, and His timing is always right.
- **The best is yet to come.** For the believer, God has kept the good wine until last. Whatever you're walking through now, the marriage supper of the Lamb is ahead.

### Questions for reflection

- Is there an area of your life where you've "run out"? Have you told Jesus about it?
- What has He told you to do that you've been questioning instead of obeying?
- Have you ever personally gone to Jesus and asked Him to make your life new?
