This is the class where you learn that translating is a lot harder than just knowing two languages.

## TLDR

- One of the best classes I took at Duke. Jam-packed with surprises
- You practice both **translation** (written) and **interpretation** (oral), in both directions
- Spanish → English is easy. English → Spanish is where the real challenge is
- You will translate a song and **sing it**. Cringe, but so useful
- Small class (about 15 people) with two instructors
- If you're thinking about it, take it

## Translation vs. Interpretation

First things first, because I didn't know this going in: **translation and interpretation are not the same thing.**

- **Translation** is written. You get time, a dictionary, and a chance to revise.
- **Interpretation** is oral. Someone is talking, and you have to get their message across in the other language right now.

The course gave you practice with both, in both directions.

## What You Actually Learn

It's been a while since I took this course, so some of the details are fuzzy. But here's what I remember, and a lot of it stuck with me:

- Translating from Spanish to English and from English to Spanish
- Different levels of translation: sticking close to the literal meaning versus taking more creative freedom to get the message across
- Readings on how some things just lose their meaning in translation, because there is no good translation for them
- Consecutive interpretation, where the speaker pauses and you repeat what they said in the other language
- Simultaneous interpretation, where you interpret live with no pauses
- Subtitling

And the assignments were all over the place, in the best way:

- **Translating an ad,** where the message and the vibe matter more than the exact words
- **Translating a song and then SINGING IT.** AHHHHHHH. More on that below
- **A comic** to translate
- **A picture book project,** where we wrote and translated our own book
- **Interpreting a FIFA corruption video**
- **Writing subtitles for a *Modern Family* clip**

## What Makes It Hard

### English to Spanish

Translating something from Spanish to English was way easier for me. The real challenge was going from English to Spanish. Finding a way to get a message across in Spanish, so it sounds natural and not like a word-for-word copy, was so hard.

Some context: I'm a native Spanish speaker, but I tell people my Spanish is *"el rancho"* Spanish, lol. I learned it from my parents, who only had a 3rd-grade education at most. So my vocabulary isn't as academic as some of the other students at Duke.

But dang it, I've got killer pronunciation. At least I don't sound like a *"yo no sabo,"* lol.

### Interpreting

Interpreting is hard. Like, *stupid* hard.

Consecutive interpretation, where the speaker pauses and you repeat what they said in the other language, was the one I was used to hearing growing up in church. Even that takes a lot of focus.

But simultaneous interpretation, where you interpret live with no pauses? Sheesh. You're listening to the next sentence while you're still saying the last one.

## What Makes It Special

### The song

Yes, you translate a song and then you sing it. It was cringe. But it was also so useful. A song forces you to care about rhythm, rhyme, and how many syllables fit in a line, not just meaning. You can't get away with a literal translation when it has to fit the melody.

### The guest speaker

We had a guest speaker who demoed live interpretation, and MY GOODNESS, she's good. Watching someone do in real time what we were struggling to do with pauses was humbling.

### The Durham courthouse

We got to go to the Durham courthouse and talk to a court interpreter. She looked very tired, but she really emphasized how needed interpreters are. In a courtroom, someone's understanding of their own case depends on that interpreter.

She also taught us some of the note-taking and scribbling methods interpreters use to keep up with a speaker. They're wild.

### The class itself

The class was small, around 15 people, and it had **two instructors**: Melissa Simmermyer and Joan Munne. Both were great instructors, for real. I remember just staying after class to chat with them.

That semester, I was also in CS 201, which [did not go great](/writing/duke-courses/data-structures-cs201). Between the chaos in that class and all the doubts I was feeling, this 15-person class was a real treat. It was the class I looked forward to.

## Who Should Take It

Anyone who wants to take it. Seriously.

- **If you grew up speaking Spanish at home,** this class will show you how much you already know and how much more there is to learn. You don't need a perfect academic vocabulary. I didn't have one.
- **If you're curious about translation or interpretation as a field,** this is a great look at how much skill it takes and how much it's needed.
- **If you just want a class that's fun, small, and full of surprises,** this is it.

## Final Take

This was one of the best classes I had at Duke. It was jam-packed with surprises, there was so much opportunity to grow, and it gave me a real appreciation for the field of translation and interpretation. Next time you see an interpreter at church, in court, or on TV, know that what they're doing is stupid hard.

I loved every second of it, and I'd recommend it to anyone.
