## TLDR
As the title of the course implies this is 250++. If you liked 250, you're in for a treat.

## What You Actually Learn
These are roughly the topics:
- Understanding terminology like: IPC, CPI, throughput
- Amdalh's Law
- Wide processors
- Out of Order Execution
- Branch Prediction, Predication
- Cache Coherence Intro
- Guest Lecturers from Nvidia, Berkley

## What Makes It Hard

It truly depends how difficult you want to make your life based on the project. You can read more about my [Floating Point Approximate Adder project](https://ismaeldiaz.com/projects/floating-point-approximate-adder), but my partner and I started off with something a bit more ambitious than was realistic. We settled on an admittedly simpler project but, given the difficulty of our semesters, I just couldn't afford to go with something that was also ambitious. It also feels a bit difficult to ask students to just create an innovation to a processor in two months. While there was the option to reproduce a paper, it definitely didn't sound like that was encouraged.

The homeworks had their difficulties as well and it wasn't even the concepts, it was understanding the question. I spent more time than I care to admit asking TAs how the homework wanted me to show my solution. I learned the hard way (through some rough grades) that I need to state my assumptions so that they can take fewer points off. Overall, homeworks were a bit rough and I recommend not snoozing on them. Start early so you have time to ask TAs on Ed or go to office hours.

One topic that I really struggled to get to click was Cache Coherence. There's a primer that is recommended for us to read. I won't be earning any good favor by saying this... but I didn't like it. My biggest complaint is the diagrams. They're a bit unclear and the descriptions where the primer attempts to explain what you are seeing just didn't click for me (and this could be a me problem. I'm not a great reader). But this is all I'll say on the matter since I think I'm on the unpopular side of this fence.

You know what else will make me unpopular? The textbook we use for this class. It's the "great" Patterson and Hennessy. I have immense beef with the textbook problems. I already thought the homeworks for the course were a bit unclear, but the textbook questions are just straight up bad. They have a bad habit of leaving out key details and information that change what a solution could look like.

However, textbook questions are no reason to dislike a textbook. My bigger issue is that I rarely found myself excited to read it. Too often I'd flip a page and find another wall of text. Now you might say, "Duh! It's a textbook." I don't think that's a good excuse. Some of the best technical resources I've ever used are packed with diagrams, examples, visual intuition, and little sanity checks that help you convince yourself you actually understand what's going on. When I'm learning something complicated, I want opportunities to stop and think, "Okay, do I actually get this?" I didn't feel like I got that very often from this book.

That said, this could absolutely just be a me thing. Plenty of people swear by Patterson and Hennessy. I just never really clicked with it the way I hoped I would.

## What Makes It Special

It's computer architecture! I won't pretend to say every topic caught my attention, but the same awe I felt when I first learned about pipelining really carried over once we started talking about superscalar processors, out-of-order execution, branch prediction, and all the tricks modern CPUs use to squeeze out performance. There will be no shortage of topics to keep you interested, and the professor is very inviting to questions.

And while I ripped on the course project a little bit, it is probably the most unique part of the class. There aren't many courses where you're basically told, "Go try something interesting and see if it works." Even if your idea ends up being less ambitious than what you originally envisioned (ask me how I know), you still walk away with a much deeper appreciation for how difficult computer architecture research actually is. Reading a paper and thinking "that's neat" is one thing. Trying to reproduce or extend an idea yourself is another.

## Who Should Take It

If you're looking to do a 4+1 and need a good class to fill those grad course slots, I'd say this is a good one. If you want to do any sort of research in computer architecture, you kind of need this class to be familiar with any of the terminology that gets thrown around.

More importantly, take this class if ECE 250 left you wanting more. A lot of courses build on prerequisite material because they have to. This one genuinely feels like a sequel. Instead of learning that modern processors do clever things, you spend the semester learning what those clever things actually are.

## Final Take

Be ready to have to do a decent amount of work for your project while also balancing homeworks, a midterm, and a final.

Take this class though. Especially if you were at least amused by 250. You will not be disappointed.

This is one of those classes where I occasionally walked out of lecture and immediately wanted to go read more about whatever we had just talked about. That's usually a good sign.
