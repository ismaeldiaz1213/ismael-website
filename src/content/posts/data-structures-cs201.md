This is the class where you learn data structures. Duh.

## TLDR

- **If you've written a single line of code before, take 201.** Skip 101.
- You learn the classic data structures in Java: linked lists, trees, hash sets, hash maps, priority queues, and more
- The data structures part was explained well. The algorithms part never clicked for me
- Exams were fair, but very, very wordy
- Projects were applicable but strange, and some were flat-out confusing (looking at you, Huffman)
- The course was way too nice about deadlines and grades
- CS is toxic. I heard that going in and thought I was ready. I wasn't

## Picking the Right Entry Course

My beef with this class started before I even took it.

Coming in, I was torn between ECE and staying in CS, and I had no idea which intro course to start with. Do I start at CS 101? Do I jump straight to 201? Am I even ready for 201? There was no real way to know. No placement test, no clear guidance. Just Reddit threads and vibes.

So let me save you the Reddit search. **IF YOU'VE WRITTEN A SINGLE LINE OF CODE BEFORE, TAKE 201.** CS 101, or god forbid EGR 103 (now EGR 105), is pointless if you already have some programming knowledge. Having seen what 101 covers, I don't think it would have filled the gaps I ended up with anyway.

## What You Actually Learn

The course is taught in Java. Here's a sample of what it covers, though there are probably things I'm missing:

- Runtime complexity (Big-O)
- Manipulating strings
- "Loop invariants" (I still have no idea what that means to this day)
- Recursion
- Linked lists
- Trees
- Hash sets and hash maps
- Priority queues
- Greedy algorithms
- Dijkstra's algorithm
- Sorting (sort of, more on that below)

Each week also comes with **APTs**: small, autograded coding problems. You get five a week, and one of them is a challenge problem.

## What Makes It Hard

I'm going to be frank: this course sucked for me. Part of that is a skill issue on my end, and part of it is how things were presented.

### The lectures

Lectures were hard for me to follow, and a lot of the time they just didn't make sense to me. When we covered runtime, it felt like code was read out loud and the runtime was announced at the end. I just... I don't get how that helps me. I needed to see *how* you get from the code to the Big-O, step by step, and I never really got that.

To be fair, the data structures themselves were explained well. I understood what a linked list or a hash map *was*. But when it came to the algorithms portion, things just didn't click.

And seriously, they need to find a better way to teach a priority queue.

### The exams

The exams were fair, but very wordy. And a decent number of questions gave me that feeling of *"aww crap."* You read the question, you read it again, and you realize you have no idea how to even start. That feeling showed up more often than I'd like to admit.

### The gaps

So much about this class felt disjointed, and the parts I thought would be necessary for me just... weren't covered. We never actually implemented sorting algorithms. That came back to bite me later: in ECE 250, I struggled to write insertion sort in C for the first project.

The projects mostly told you exactly what to do in the instructions, so you could finish them without really understanding the *why*. And some of them were just confusing. That Huffman coding project still doesn't make sense to me.

To this day, I still have gaps that I feel should have been covered in 201.

### The course was too nice

This might sound strange after all that complaining, but I think the course was way too nice.

Extra late days. Soft deadlines. Curving like crazy. Softening on the numbers. It feels great in the moment, but actions have consequences, and a class that cushions every one of them doesn't prepare you for anything that comes after it. Nobody is going to curve your job interview.

### The people

About half the people in that course seemed like experts already.

I kid you not, in week 2 I sat next to a guy who told me he had finished all the APTs in one night. Mind you, there are 5 APTs a week, one of them is a challenge, and the semester is 16 weeks long. Somehow this guy did all 5 × 16 of them???? Why are you even in this class, man?

That's the reality of CS. Some people have been programming since they were 10, with parents who are software engineers and taught them a college degree's worth of material before they ever stepped on campus. That's not the course's fault, and it's not anyone's fault. It's just how it is.

### The isolation

I'd heard that CS was toxic. I thought I was ready for it. I wasn't actually prepared for when it happened.

The class is massive, and discussion sections were always so awkward. Picture this: it's 8:30 in the morning, you're sitting with people you don't know, and you have to figure out how to write a recursive tree algorithm. Like bro... what?

On top of that, coming from an Aldine education and a low-income community, the culture shock hit hard. I'd overhear people talking about their weekend plans for some boating trip, or other random stuff that I just couldn't relate to. None of it was aimed at me. But between the experts, the conversations, and the "aww crap" exam questions, I felt really isolated in that class.

## What Makes It Special

I'll give credit where it's due:

- **The data structures were explained well.** If you come out of 201 knowing nothing else, you'll at least know when to reach for a hash map.
- **The exams were fair.** Wordy, so practice reading carefully under time pressure, but fair.
- **The projects were applicable.** Strange and not all that interesting, but they did connect to real things.

And getting through this class is what got me to [ECE 250](/writing/duke-courses/computer-architecture-ece250-2), which ended up being one of my favorite classes at Duke.

## Who Should Take It

If you're going into CS or ECE, you have to take it anyway. So the real question is *when*.

- **Any programming experience at all?** Go straight to 201. Don't bother with 101, EGR 103, or EGR 105.
- **Having a bad time?** Here's the honest truth: get used to it. If you have a bad time in data structures and algorithms, that feeling doesn't go away later in CS. It never gets better. Ever.

CS is not for everyone, and it doesn't *need* to be for everyone. If you can't handle it, don't do it. There's no shame in that.

If you do stay, worry about yourself. There will be a competitive spirit in you that says "I need to know this, and this, and this," and you'll feel like you need to catch up to that guy who finished every APT in one night. The reality is, **you never will.** More on that in a later post.

## What This Class Taught Me About Myself

A lot of realities sank in with this course, and I think they're worth mentioning.

I'm stubborn enough to never take no for an answer. I'd rather complain and push forward than quit, and I did push forward. But CS 330 was basically a repeat of this class for me, and that's when I learned something about myself: when I feel trapped, it's hard for me to get out of the spiral.

So I settled for "good enough."

In my head, I was a low-income, first-gen kid. I had already made it just by being at Duke. What more could I ask of myself?

It wasn't until now, as I'm looking for a job, thinking about ways to start businesses, and trying to do more for my community, that I've come to regret that freshman mentality. "I already made it" was a ceiling I put on myself, and nobody in that classroom put it there but me.

## If I Could Change One Thing

I'm not here to blame the course for everything, but a couple of things would have helped:

- **A real placement check.** A short self-assessment that tells you "start at 101" or "start at 201" would beat Reddit threads by a mile.
- **Walk through runtime analysis step by step.** Count the operations line by line instead of reading the code and stating the answer.
- **Show the algorithms, don't just tell them.** Visual walkthroughs of priority queues, Dijkstra's, and Huffman trees would go a long way.
- **Hold the line on deadlines and grades.** Fewer cushions, more honest feedback about where you actually stand.

## Final Take

CS 201 was rough for me. Part of it was me, part of it was how the material was presented, and part of it was a culture I wasn't ready for, even though I'd been warned. I came out of it with real gaps, a bruised ego, and a habit of settling for "good enough" that took me years to notice.

If you're about to take it, take it seriously, worry about yourself, and don't settle the way I did.
